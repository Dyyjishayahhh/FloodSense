P0065	Normal	INTRODUCTION
P0067	Normal	Flooding remains one of the most persistent hazards affecting Philippine communities. Its consequences extend beyond inundation and property damage to interrupted travel, disrupted livelihoods, displacement, and difficult household decisions before and during severe weather. National disaster statistics continue to document the effects of extreme events on Filipino families and communities (Philippine Statistics Authority [PSA], 2026). These conditions make accurate, understandable, and appropriately qualified risk information an essential part of disaster preparedness.
P0068	Normal	Bacoor City, Cavite, includes low-lying and river-connected areas exposed to recurring flood conditions. Flood-mitigation works in the Imus River Basin were established to reduce peak floodwater volume in Cavite, including low portions of Bacoor and its tributaries (Japan International Cooperation Agency [JICA], 2021). Local interviews also identified historical flood records, geographic materials, preparedness procedures, and evacuation information held by city offices, together with restrictions on the availability and public use of machine-readable spatial data.
P0069	Normal	Residents may obtain flood information through official social-media pages, barangay announcements, text messages, broadcast media, websites, community networks, and personal observation. P0070	Normal	Research on flood-risk communication emphasizes that technical information is most useful when users can understand its geographic meaning, source, limitation, and connection to appropriate preparedness action (Mostafiz et al., 2022). Geographic provenance is also necessary because a technically processed boundary or map does not automatically become institutionally verified hazard information (Tullis & Kar, 2021). In Bacoor City, the absence of an approved operational susceptibility dataset and the need for local expert validation remain central constraints.
P0071	Normal	Against this context, the study examines a scenario-based approach to pre-event flood-susceptibility assessment and preparedness support. The approach keeps a deterministic assessment separate from decision-support guidance, requires the user to confirm a hypothetical rainfall scenario and location, and discloses that results are not live observations, official forecasts, warnings, or evacuation orders.
P0072	Normal	FloodSense is developed as an Android resident application and a separate Web Administration System that organize these verified concepts. The current prototype demonstrates rule-based assessment, explanation, interactive geographic presentation, structured preparedness guidance, resident account controls, and governed administrative workflows. P0086	Normal	Theoretical Framework
P0087	Normal	The theoretical and conceptual framework of FloodSense relates user-confirmed inputs, controlled knowledge, deterministic inference, geographic presentation, and separate preparedness support. The framework uses four function-named modules so that technologies and database storage remain implementation mechanisms rather than conceptual modules.
P0088	Normal	Scenario and Location Input Module. The resident selects or confirms a hypothetical rainfall intensity and duration, chooses a supported location manually or through an optional one-time foreground location request, and confirms or corrects the proposed barangay before explicitly requesting an assessment. These inputs are not live measurements or forecasts.
P0089	Normal	Susceptibility Assessment and Explanation Module. The deterministic Rule-Based Expert System validates the confirmed inputs, matches applicable rules, resolves priorities or conflicts, and returns a Low, Moderate, High, or Very High class with a human-readable explanation. Repeatability does not establish scientific validity; the rules and local data still require provenance, expert review, and institutional authorization.
P0090	Normal	Preparedness and Evacuation Information Module. A separate structured Decision Support System uses the assessment only as context for published guidance and does not change its class or issue an evacuation order. The mobile application can request nearest-center information, but distance does not establish route safety, opening status, accessibility, or live capacity, and public use depends on authorized current records.
P0091	Normal	Governance and Data Stewardship Module. Authorized administrators use the custom Web Administration System for dashboards and controlled workflows involving map and rainfall references, parameters, DSS content and structured flows, evacuation centers and staged center imports, data sources, and audit history. Ordinary administrators do not edit raw rules, rule conditions, priorities, conflict-resolution logic, or algorithm code. Technical Django Admin remains separate. The confirmed fixed-schema Expert System parameter CSV exchange is not yet implemented.
P0092	Normal	The four modules produce an explainable, scenario-specific result, geographic presentation, and preparedness information while maintaining source, permission, validation, and limitation boundaries. The 47-barangay layer is an administrative reference rather than a susceptibility dataset, and the framework does not include real-time monitoring, autonomous alerts, official forecasts or warnings, background GPS, automatic evacuation orders, safe-route calculation, machine-learning prediction, or self-modifying rules.

P0139	Normal	Related Foreign Literature
P0140	Normal	Flood Susceptibility and Scenario-Based Assessment
P0141	Normal	Flood susceptibility expresses the relative tendency of a location to experience flooding when relevant conditions are present. It differs from flood hazard, which concerns the probability and physical characteristics of an event; exposure, which concerns people or assets in affected places; vulnerability, which concerns the capacity to be harmed; and risk, which combines hazard and potential consequences. Membele, Naidu, and Mutanga (2022) found that flood-vulnerability mapping in developing countries varies substantially in terminology, indicators, spatial scale, data availability, and validation practice. Their review shows why a public system must name the type of assessment it performs and avoid presenting a general vulnerability or hazard map as a locally validated susceptibility conclusion.
P0142	Normal	Scenario-based assessment supports planning by holding selected conditions explicit instead of implying that the system is observing an event as it occurs. A hypothetical rainfall intensity and duration can be treated as assessment inputs only when their meanings, permitted combinations, and geographic applicability are documented. This supports the FloodSense interaction in which the resident selects or confirms a scenario before requesting assessment. It does not establish that the scenario is current, predicted, or officially issued, and it does not justify importing weights or thresholds derived for another place.
P0143	Normal	Deterministic Rule-Based Expert Systems and Knowledge Bases
P0144	Normal	A Rule-Based Expert System represents domain knowledge through facts, conditions, and production rules and applies an inference procedure to derive a conclusion. Buchanan and Shortliffe (1984) provide the foundational distinction between the knowledge base and the inference mechanism. More recently, Papadopoulos et al. (2022) reviewed technologies and standards used in rule-based decision-support systems and described how encoded knowledge and rules engines evaluate case-specific facts. Although their review concerns clinical systems, the architectural distinction is relevant to FloodSense: flood knowledge, scenario facts, geographic facts, and explanations belong to controlled knowledge structures, while the inference procedure determines which eligible rule applies.
P0145	Normal	Deterministic processing means that the same approved facts and the same published rule version should produce the same result. It does not imply that the rules are scientifically valid merely because the software executes them consistently. Knowledge acquisition, conflict handling, parameter definitions, version control, and expert or institutional validation remain necessary. Accordingly, ordinary FloodSense administrators may manage approved records and permitted values but do not rewrite raw rule conditions, priorities, conflict-resolution logic, or algorithm code. The confirmed fixed-schema CSV exchange is a future controlled-value workflow and is not evidence that such exchange is currently operational.
P0147	Normal	Explainable Inference
P0148	Normal	Explainability is necessary when a system produces a classification that may influence preparedness decisions. Kostopoulos, Davrazos, and Kotsiantis (2024) reviewed explainable decision-support approaches and emphasized transparency, interpretability, and human-understandable justification. Their review primarily addresses artificial-intelligence-based systems, but its communication principles apply to deterministic inference: a user should be able to understand the relevant inputs, the basis of the result, and the limitations of the evidence without being required to inspect source code or raw rule definitions.
P0149	Normal	For FloodSense, an explanation should identify the confirmed rainfall scenario, resolved geographic context, susceptibility class, applicable basis, source or version information, and any limitation state. This supports accountability while preserving the distinction between explanation and rule administration. It also prevents a color or label from appearing authoritative without disclosing what it represents. Explainability does not remove the need for validated data, and a clear explanation cannot turn provisional information into an official local assessment.
P0151	Normal	Decision Support and Preparedness Guidance
P0152	Normal	A Decision Support System organizes information, alternatives, or recommended actions to assist human judgment. In flood preparedness, support may include understandable mitigation choices, household preparation, communication planning, protection of documents and valuables, assistance for vulnerable household members, attention to official advisories, and evacuation readiness. Mostafiz et al. (2022) observed that many web-based flood tools provide technical information but do not consistently provide locally actionable information for individuals and communities. Their review supports combining understandable risk communication with practical guidance while clearly stating the tool's authority and limitations.
P0153	Normal	FloodSense separates the susceptibility assessment from preparedness guidance. The deterministic Expert System produces the assessment, while the DSS presents source-backed pre-event guidance without changing the susceptibility class. Information about evacuation facilities may support readiness when its source, verification date, location, and operational limitations are known, but distance alone does not prove route safety, current opening, accessibility, or available capacity. The DSS therefore assists preparation and does not issue an official warning, evacuation order, or guarantee of safety.
P0154	Normal	GIS, Interactive Maps, and Spatial Databases
P0155	Normal	Geographic Information Systems organize, analyze, and present information associated with locations. Their value in flood research depends on the suitability of the layers, coordinate reference systems, spatial resolution, classification methods, and validation procedures. An administrative boundary can resolve the name of a barangay or provide map context, but it contains no flood-susceptibility fact unless appropriate hazard or susceptibility evidence has been associated with it. This distinction is important when users may interpret any colored polygon as an official hazard layer.
P0156	Normal	Spatial databases support the controlled storage and querying of geographic records. The PostGIS Development Group (2026) explains that PostGIS extends PostgreSQL with geometry and geography types, spatial indexes, spatial relationships, coordinate transformation, validity operations, and spatial processing. These capabilities are relevant to point-in-polygon queries, administrative-area lookup, map-feature storage, and provenance-linked geographic records. They provide technical infrastructure, not scientific validation: a valid geometry and a successful spatial query do not establish that a flood conclusion is institutionally approved.
P0158	Normal	Flood-Risk Communication
P0159	Normal	Flood-risk information must be understandable, actionable, and explicit about uncertainty. Mostafiz et al. (2022) identified gaps in web-based flood communication, particularly where tools provide information without sufficiently connecting it to decisions that residents can make. Clear terms, legends, labels, explanation text, and source notices help prevent non-specialists from treating model outputs as certain predictions. Communication should also distinguish long-term or scenario-based information from time-sensitive official warnings.
P0160	Normal	These principles support a guided FloodSense flow rather than an unexplained technical map. Susceptibility colors must be paired with text, the selected scenario, and limitations. Preparedness information must direct users toward official channels during actual events. The interface must not imply that a neutral boundary layer, a demonstration color, or a provisional record represents a current flood condition.
P0162	Normal	Data Provenance and Geographic Validation
P0163	Normal	Provenance records where information originated, how it was transformed, which version was used, and what review or approval applies. Tullis and Kar (2021) connected provenance to ethical replicability and reproducibility in GIScience, including critical applications in which geographic data affect public decisions. Fakhruddin et al. (2022) similarly emphasized risk-informed data practices and the importance of understanding how disaster and climate data are produced, shared, interpreted, and governed.
P0164	Normal	For FloodSense, provenance should accompany susceptibility parameters, geographic layers, preparedness items, and resource information. Technical geometry validation may confirm coordinate-system consistency, valid shapes, or feature counts, but institutional validation concerns authority, currency, meaning, and permitted public use. The two forms of validation are related but not interchangeable. This distinction prevents a technically processed dataset from being represented as official Bacoor City flood information without documented approval.
P0166	Normal	Foreground Location and Privacy
P0167	Normal	Mobile location can reveal a person's position and may contribute to identification or tracking. Android Developers (2026) distinguishes foreground access, one-time access, and background access and recommends requesting only the permission needed for the active function. This supports a FloodSense flow in which location is obtained only after a user initiates location selection or assessment, while manual selection remains available when the resident declines permission or prefers another area.
P0168	Normal	Foreground acquisition does not imply continuous monitoring. FloodSense does not require background GPS tracking, automatic polling, or autonomous location-based alerts. Location handling should remain purpose-limited to the active assessment, use only the precision necessary for the supported geographic operation, and avoid retaining unnecessary movement information. The presentation must also allow the user to confirm or correct the detected barangay because device accuracy and boundary proximity can affect geographic resolution.

The model can guide the selection of criteria for FloodSense, but it does not provide a ready-made acceptance result or replace an approved evaluation instrument.

P0174	Normal	Existing Foreign Flood-Information Platforms

The PSA documented the 2023 merger and renaming of barangays and currently lists 47 barangays in the City of Bacoor (PSA, 2023, 2025). These records support the current identities used in a reference layer. They do not establish the accuracy of every derived polygon, City verification of a third-party boundary, or flood conditions within any barangay. Administrative reference and flood-susceptibility evidence must therefore remain separate.
P0189	Normal	Administrative Data Governance and Responsible Use
P0190	Normal	Government publications, local records, and research datasets differ in authority and permitted use. An official list of administrative units may verify names and codes, while an authorized hazard agency may provide hazard information, and a local office may validate operational records. Combining these sources requires documentation of ownership, date, scale, processing, approval, and restrictions. Provenance is particularly important when a dataset has been transformed, because technical processing can change geometry or attributes without changing the institution responsible for the original information.
P0191	Normal	FloodSense consequently distinguishes approved, provisional, technically validated, and unsupported information. The 47-barangay layer may serve as a current administrative reference after documented derivation and geometry checks, but it is not a flood-susceptibility layer and is not automatically City-issued or City-verified. No operational susceptibility dataset should be described as established until the necessary source, method, expert review, institutional authority, and database integration are documented.
P0193	Normal	Philippine Data Privacy and Software-Quality Context

For a resident-facing location function, these principles support clear notice, user initiation, limited collection, secure handling, and avoidance of continuous background tracking. They also support role-controlled administrative access. The Act is legally authoritative despite falling outside the preferred publication period for recent literature.
P0195	Normal	Philippine software studies have also used ISO/IEC 25010 as an evaluation framework, but the criteria and edition must be identified accurately. An evaluation of another application cannot be transferred to FloodSense, and the presence of a questionnaire does not establish completed results. FloodSense quality conclusions will require its own approved instrument, respondents or evaluators, documented procedure, and completed analysis.
P0197	Normal	Existing Philippine Hazard-Information and Planning Platforms
P0198	Normal	UP NOAH remains an important Philippine reference for accessible geographic hazard information. Lagmay et al. (2017) documented the dissemination of near-real-time hazard information and flood maps through Web-GIS using open-source code and application programming interfaces. The dated publication supports discussion of public map-based risk communication, while the general platform should not be treated as a research study with an invented SDLC. UP NOAH differs from FloodSense because its documented hazard-information resources were institutionally developed, whereas FloodSense is a scenario-based research prototype and is not an official forecasting or warning platform.
P0199	Normal	HazardHunterPH is a GeoRisk Philippines application that produces site-specific indicative multi-hazard assessments from official agency information (GeoRisk Philippines, 2023). Its rapid geographic lookup and source-based reporting are relevant to FloodSense's emphasis on accessible information, provenance, and visible limitations. It remains an official platform rather than a substitute for locally validated FloodSense rules or data, and its reports do not confer forecasting or warning authority on FloodSense.
P0200	Normal	GeoMapperPH supports governed collection of hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and coping-capacity information by trained government and agency users, while PlanSmartPH Ready to Rebuild organizes information for local-government rehabilitation and recovery planning (DOST-PHIVOLCS, 2022a, 2022b). These platforms illustrate structured data governance and institutional workflows. They are discussed as existing Philippine systems, not retained as Related Studies, because the inspected official pages do not provide a complete research methodology and results section suitable for the comparative study tables.
P0201	Normal	Related Foreign Studies
P0202	Normal	Examining Flood Vulnerability Mapping Approaches in Developing Countries: A Scoping Review
P0203	Normal	Membele, Naidu, and Mutanga (2022) examined how flood-vulnerability mapping was conducted in developing countries. Following PRISMA-ScR procedures, they searched Web of Science and Scopus for 2010-2020 publications, screened 2,549 records, and reviewed 130 eligible studies. They found frequent use of GIS with indicator-based and multicriteria methods, but limited stakeholder participation, sensitivity analysis, and validation, together with recurring problems involving coarse or outdated data.
P0204	Normal	The review is relevant because it shows that mapping terminology, indicators, spatial scale, participation, and validation must be reported before a flood-related output can be interpreted responsibly. Like FloodSense, the reviewed work concerns geographically organized flood information; however, it synthesizes vulnerability-mapping studies rather than building a resident scenario-assessment application. Its findings identify methodological weaknesses but do not supply Bacoor-specific factors, weights, rules, or validation, and the review was limited to studies that produced a flood-vulnerability map.
P0205	Normal	Actionable Information in Flood Risk Communications and the Potential for New Web-Based Tools for Long-Term Planning for Individuals and Community
P0206	Normal	Mostafiz, Rohli, Friedland, and Lee (2022) investigated how flood-risk information could better support long-term decisions by individuals and communities. They reviewed flood communication through mass media, citizen science, and social media and examined 50 freely available web-based flood-risk portals. Their review found that many tools emphasize short-term information, while micro-scale, actionable, long-term planning information and explicit cost-benefit support remain limited.
P0207	Normal	The study supports FloodSense's use of understandable result explanations and separate preparedness guidance. Both emphasize that technical flood information must be connected to actions a user can understand; however, the study evaluated a cross-section of portals rather than a deterministic Philippine susceptibility system. Its portal sample was a time-bounded snapshot, and its findings cannot establish what information Bacoor residents need without local survey and stakeholder evidence.
P0208	Normal	Accelerated and Interpretable Flood Susceptibility Mapping Through Explainable Deep Learning with Hydrological Prior Knowledge
P0209	Normal	Wang, Sanderson, Iqbal, and Woo (2025) developed and evaluated an explainable deep-learning approach for flood-susceptibility mapping in a 1,516-square-kilometer area of Northumberland, United Kingdom. They combined official flood records and terrain and environmental variables, compared support-vector-machine, decision-tree, random-forest, standard U-Net, and hydrology-aware U-Net models, used spatial five-fold cross-validation, and applied Grad-CAM interpretation. The proposed model achieved a reported AUC of 0.966 and faster convergence than the standard U-Net.
P0210	Normal	The study demonstrates how susceptibility models can be evaluated and interpreted, but it also reports static inputs, an inventory that excluded surface-water flooding, and the need for retraining or fine-tuning in other regions. It is relevant to FloodSense's concerns with susceptibility and explanation, yet it differs fundamentally because FloodSense uses deterministic rules rather than machine-learning prediction. The model, features, and reported performance cannot be transferred to Bacoor City without local data, validation, and a different approved method.
P0211	Normal	Related Local Studies
P0212	Normal	GIS-Based Mapping of Flashflood Susceptible Areas in Bukang Liwayway, Kibawe, Bukidnon
P0213	Normal	Puno et al. (2022) generated a flashflood-susceptibility map for Barangay Bukang Liwayway, Kibawe, Bukidnon using GIS and frequency-ratio analysis. The researchers collected 160 flashflood inventory points and evaluated nine conditioning factors, including terrain, drainage, land cover, vegetation, and rainfall, using satellite, elevation, rainfall, and field information. They reported that 89.8% of the area fell within low, 4.4% within moderate, and 5.7% within high susceptibility, with 25 households exposed to the high category.
P0214	Normal	The study is similar to FloodSense in producing spatially organized susceptibility information and emphasizing local planning, but its statistical frequency-ratio method and rural geographic context differ from FloodSense's deterministic, user-requested scenario flow. Its sample points, factor relationships, weights, land-cover conditions, and proposed evacuation-site analysis are specific to Bukang Liwayway. They cannot be generalized to Bacoor City without locally appropriate data, expert review, field validation, and institutional authorization.
P0215	Normal	Flood Risk Assessment Using GIS-Based Analytical Hierarchy Process in the Municipality of Odiongan, Romblon, Philippines
P0216	Normal	Gacu, Monjardin, Senoro, and Tan (2022) assessed flood risk in Odiongan, Romblon using a GIS-based analytical hierarchy process under the Sendai Framework. The study combined five hazard, nine vulnerability, and three exposure parameters, obtained comparative judgments from ten experts and end-users, created weighted-overlay maps, and checked results against ground assessment and historical events. It identified nine barangays with high flood risk and reported that 3.26% of the municipality was in the high-risk class.
P0217	Normal	The work demonstrates a documented approach for combining spatial criteria, expert judgment, and validation. It is relevant to FloodSense's emphasis on geographic evidence and expert review, but it assesses risk by combining hazard, vulnerability, and exposure rather than producing FloodSense's bounded susceptibility output. Its judgments and weights are subjective and locally derived, so they cannot be reused as Bacoor rules or parameters without renewed local evidence and authorization.
P0218	Normal	Web-Based Solution for Flood Warning Decision Support in the Province of Leyte, Philippines
P0219	Normal	Bentoso et al. (2021) developed a sensor-based engineering prototype intended to support flood-warning decisions in the Province of Leyte. The system collected rainfall and water-level readings, transmitted the data to a web interface, presented graphical information, and used thresholds to send warning messages; the authors also reported functional testing of the prototype. The documented output was a working monitoring and alert interface for responsible users, not evidence of province-wide operational deployment.
P0220	Normal	The Leyte study is relevant because it separates sensed conditions, decision support, and warning communication within a digital flood application. It differs from FloodSense because it relies on continuous sensors and alert thresholds, whereas FloodSense has no continuous monitoring, automatic polling, autonomous alerts, or official warning authority. Its sensor placement, communications, thresholds, and local results cannot be transferred to Bacoor City without new scientific, technical, and institutional validation.
P0221	Normal	Synthesis of the Reviewed Literature
P0222	Normal	The reviewed literature and studies show that flood susceptibility, vulnerability, hazard, exposure, and risk are related but not interchangeable. Membele et al. (2022) identified wide variation in mapping terminology, indicators, scale, participation, and validation, while Puno et al. (2022) and Gacu et al. (2022) demonstrated how local studies combine geographic factors, documented analytical procedures, and validation. Their methods support disciplined geographic assessment, but their factor weights, classifications, and findings cannot be imported into Bacoor City because the study areas, data, scales, and intended outputs differ.
P0223	Normal	Mostafiz et al. (2022) showed that flood information is more useful when it supports understandable action, and official platforms such as UP NOAH and HazardHunterPH illustrate public geographic communication with visible institutional sources. FloodSense therefore separates its deterministic susceptibility result from preparedness guidance and identifies the scenario, location, source, and limitations presented to the user. Bentoso et al. (2021) demonstrates a different approach based on sensors and warning messages; its contribution is relevant to flood decision support but does not justify adding real-time monitoring or autonomous alerts to FloodSense.
P0224	Normal	Explainability and validation remain distinct responsibilities. Wang et al. (2025) showed that an interpretable machine-learning model can be evaluated quantitatively, but its static inputs, inventory limitations, and retraining requirement also show why performance in one area does not establish validity elsewhere. FloodSense instead uses deterministic rule matching so that the same approved facts and rule version produce the same result, yet repeatability alone does not validate the rules. Geographic provenance, local expert review, institutional authorization, and appropriate Bacoor data remain necessary.
P0225	Normal	Across the sources, geographic technologies support storage, analysis, visualization, and communication, but technical capability does not establish scientific or institutional authority. Administrative boundaries can support location resolution without containing flood-susceptibility facts. Official platforms, reviewed studies, and FloodSense also serve different users and disaster-management phases; therefore, similarities in mapping or decision support must not be interpreted as equivalence in data, functions, or authority.
P0226	Normal	Research Gap
P0227	Normal	Existing research already contributes flood-susceptibility mapping, GIS-based multicriteria assessment, explainable modeling, risk communication, sensor-based warning, and public hazard platforms. The inspected evidence nevertheless leaves a context-specific need for public-facing, location-specific, scenario-based susceptibility information that clearly explains a deterministic result and then presents preparedness guidance as a separate form of support. This is not a claim that FloodSense is the first flood application or that no comparable system exists.
P0228	Normal	The remaining gap also concerns provenance and transferability. The reviewed studies do not provide an approved set of Bacoor City susceptibility factors, rule thresholds, validated geographic inputs, or preparedness content that can be adopted automatically. External formulas, weights, model performance, sensor thresholds, and map classifications were developed for other data and places. FloodSense therefore requires local expert and institutional validation and remains a pre-event research prototype rather than an official forecasting, warning, evacuation, or hazard-certification system.
