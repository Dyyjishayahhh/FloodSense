P0065	Normal	INTRODUCTION
P0066	Normal	Flooding remains one of the most persistent hazards affecting Philippine communities. Beyond physical inundation, its effects include interrupted travel, disrupted work and schooling, household displacement, property loss, and difficult decisions about preparation and evacuation. These consequences make understandable and appropriately qualified flood information an important part of community preparedness.
P0067	Normal	Bacoor City includes low-lying and river-connected communities that experience recurring flood conditions. Flood-mitigation works in the Imus River Basin were established to reduce peak floodwater volume in parts of Cavite, including low portions of Bacoor and its tributaries (Japan International Cooperation Agency [JICA], 2021). City offices also maintain historical, geographic, preparedness, and evacuation information, although the availability, authority, and permitted public use of particular records vary.
P0068	Normal	The initial needs-assessment survey included 40 consenting adult Bacoor City residents from 14 barangays who had experienced flooding or serious heavy-rain effects. Thirty respondents (75.0%) reported more than five such events during the preceding three years. Reported effects included disruption of work or study (97.5%), flooding of streets or community areas (87.5%), difficult travel routes (85.0%), floodwater entering the home (77.5%), and evacuation or temporary relocation (60.0%).
P0069	Normal	Access to usable information was also a recurring concern. Twenty-nine respondents (72.5%) reported difficulty obtaining needed flood information, and 30 (75.0%) stated that inadequate information often or always affected their decisions. Thirty respondents (75.0%) found it difficult, very difficult, or were unsure how to determine the susceptibility of their location, while 33 (82.5%) reported the same difficulty regarding verified evacuation-center information. The highest-rated information problems concerned information that was too general for the respondent's location, limited access when internet service or electricity was unreliable, overly general preparedness advice, and unclear source or date information.
P0070	Normal	The survey also showed that 39 respondents (97.5%) had access to an Android device and that 33 (82.5%) were comfortable with one-time GPS access when checking an area. Manual barangay selection and temporary map-pin use were also acceptable to most respondents, while 75.0% preferred information presented in both English and Filipino. These findings support a user-initiated mobile flow while also emphasizing privacy, manual alternatives, and clear communication.
P0071	Normal	Within this context, FloodSense is a scenario-based, pre-event flood-susceptibility assessment and preparedness-support system. It requires the resident to confirm a hypothetical rainfall scenario and location and to request an assessment explicitly. A deterministic Rule-Based Expert System produces an explained susceptibility result, while a separate Decision Support System presents preparedness guidance. FloodSense does not replace official observations, forecasts, warnings, emergency directives, or local-government authority.
P0072	Normal	Statement of the Problem
P0073	Normal	Recurring flooding in Bacoor City affects household routines, mobility, work, schooling, property, and evacuation decisions. The initial survey establishes that residents repeatedly experience these effects and that many have difficulty obtaining information that is location-specific, understandable, timely, and traceable to a source. Local institutional records and interviews further show that geographic and preparedness information must be handled according to its ownership, validation status, and permitted public use.
P0074	Normal	The central research problem is therefore the limited availability of an accessible and appropriately qualified way for residents to examine how a hypothetical rainfall situation may relate to the susceptibility of a selected Bacoor City location and then obtain separate preparedness guidance. Addressing this problem requires attention to resident information needs, deterministic and explainable assessment, geographic presentation, privacy, data provenance, administrative governance, technical verification, and product-quality evaluation.

P0077	Normal	2. What knowledge, rainfall-scenario, geographic, privacy, provenance, preparedness, evacuation-information, and administrative-governance requirements are necessary for FloodSense?

P0080	Normal	5. How will the relevant product-quality characteristics of FloodSense be evaluated using an approved ISO/IEC 25010-based instrument and defined evaluator groups?

P0094	Normal	Theoretical Framework
P0095	Normal	The theoretical framework of FloodSense connects resident access, user-confirmed inputs, deterministic inference, geographic presentation, separate preparedness guidance, and governed information management. Consistent with the study's research problems, related functions are consolidated under five function-named modules rather than being separated according to individual technologies.
P0096	Normal	User Access and Authentication Module. This module controls resident and administrator identity, registration, sign-in, verification, password recovery, privacy and legal acceptance, profiles, roles, and permissions. Authentication is necessary because FloodSense processes account information, optional location data, and governed administrative records that must not be available to unauthorized users.
P0097	Normal	Scenario and Location Input Module. This module receives the resident's hypothetical rainfall intensity and duration and the selected location. The resident may choose a barangay, adjust a temporary map pin, or request one-time foreground location access, then confirm or correct the resolved barangay before requesting assessment. These inputs describe a user-selected scenario rather than a live observation or forecast.
P0098	Normal	Susceptibility Assessment and Explanation Module. This module validates the confirmed facts, applies deterministic rule matching and conflict handling, and returns a susceptibility class with a human-readable explanation and geographic presentation. The same approved facts and rule version produce the same outcome, while the scientific and local validity of the underlying knowledge remains dependent on appropriate data, provenance, expert review, and institutional authorization.
P0099	Normal	Preparedness Decision Support Module. This module presents source-backed preparedness guidance through structured questions and information flows. It may present authorized evacuation-center information and straight-line distance as reference information. The guidance remains separate from the susceptibility assessment and does not modify the assessment class or issue an evacuation order.
P0100	Normal	Administration and Data Governance Module. This module supports authenticated and permission-controlled management of rainfall and map references, assessment parameters, DSS content, evacuation-center records, data sources, review records, and audit history. Ordinary administrators do not directly modify raw rule conditions, priorities, conflict-resolution logic, or algorithm code. Technical Django Admin access remains separate and is reserved for developer or research maintenance.

P0103	Normal	The architecture places the resident on the left and the authorized administrator on the right. P0112	Normal	Scope and Limitation of the Study
P0113	Normal	FloodSense covers an Android application for Bacoor City residents and a browser-based administration system for authorized personnel. Resident users register and authenticate, accept the applicable privacy and legal notices, select or confirm a hypothetical rainfall intensity and duration, identify a location through barangay selection, a temporary map pin, or optional one-time foreground GPS, confirm or correct the resolved barangay, and explicitly request an assessment. The deterministic Rule-Based Expert System returns an explained susceptibility classification and map context, while a separate Decision Support System presents source-backed preparedness guidance and authorized evacuation-center reference information. The administration system supports authenticated dashboards and permission-controlled management of rainfall and map references, assessment parameters, preparedness content, evacuation-center records, sources, review records, and audit history. Ordinary administrators do not directly edit raw rules, rule priorities, conflict-resolution logic, or algorithm code.
P0114	Normal	However, the study is limited to scenario-based, pre-event assessment and preparedness support within Bacoor City's documented administrative coverage. It does not observe rainfall or water levels continuously and does not provide automatic rainfall updates, real-time flood monitoring, official forecasts or warnings, autonomous alerts, background GPS tracking, automatic evacuation orders, safe-road routing, live evacuation-center occupancy, or machine-learning prediction. The 47-barangay layer is used only as an administrative reference and does not itself contain flood-susceptibility information or establish City verification. The authority and local validity of susceptibility results, preparedness content, and evacuation-center records depend on appropriate source documentation, expert review, and institutional validation. Center distance is straight-line reference information and does not establish route safety, accessibility, opening status, available capacity, or safety. The Android application and web services require a compatible device and network access for connected functions. The 40-person purposive survey represents the participating respondents rather than the entire Bacoor City population, and personal and one-time location information remains subject to consent, purpose limitation, role-controlled access, and the Data Privacy Act of 2012.

P0117	Normal	Android Application. The resident-facing FloodSense software developed with Flutter. It includes authentication, legal acceptance, onboarding, scenario and location confirmation, assessment requests, map/results presentation, structured preparedness guidance, nearest-center information, preferences, and account controls. It does not provide live alerts, assessment history, fully offline operation, or official warnings.
P0118	Normal	Check My Area. The location-selection flow that allows manual area selection, placement or adjustment of a map pin, and an optional one-time foreground location request. P0119	Normal	Color-Coded Susceptibility Levels. The visual scheme used on the dynamic map in which green indicates Low, yellow indicates Moderate, orange indicates High, and red indicates Very High flood susceptibility for the active scenario.
P0120	Normal	CPDCO (City Planning and Development Coordinator Office). The Bacoor City office that maintains planning and geographic information, including elevation, land-use, flood-hazard, and barangay-boundary materials relevant to the study.
P0121	Normal	Decision Support System (DSS). The component separate from the Rule-Based Expert System that presents published preparedness guidance and structured questions using the assessment context. It does not change the susceptibility result, issue an official warning or evacuation order, or make the user's final decision.
P0122	Normal	Dynamic Map. The provider-aware interactive map that supports panning, zooming, scenario-dependent demonstration coloring, manual selection, map-pin placement, foreground location, reference boundaries, and center markers when data are available. Its display does not convert provisional data into official hazard information.
P0123	Normal	Expert-Provided and Expert-Validated Knowledge. Flood-related facts, parameters, rules, preparedness guidance, and supporting information that have been supplied or reviewed by an authorized source and recorded with adequate provenance. Repository presence or technical validation alone does not establish institutional approval.
P0124	Normal	Expert System. A knowledge-based computer system that applies stored facts and conditional rules through an inference process. In FloodSense, it produces an explainable susceptibility class for a supported geographic area and rainfall scenario.
P0125	Normal	Flood Susceptibility. The relative potential of a supported geographic area to experience rainfall-related flooding under a user-confirmed scenario. The current prototype demonstrates Low, Moderate, High, or Very High outputs but does not yet contain an approved operational Bacoor City susceptibility dataset and is not a real-time prediction.
P0126	Normal	Geographic Information System (GIS). A system for organizing, analyzing, and presenting information associated with geographic locations. FloodSense uses GIS functions for polygons, points, containment queries, interactive visualization, and evacuation-center locations.
P0127	Normal	GeoDjango. The geographic extension of Django used by FloodSense to represent spatial data and perform geographic operations between backend services and the PostGIS database.
P0128	Normal	Inference Process. The procedure that matches facts from the selected rainfall scenario and geographic area against validated rules in the knowledge base to derive a susceptibility conclusion and supporting explanation.
P0129	Normal	Knowledge Base. The structured collection of expert-validated facts, conditional rules, geographic attributes, rainfall parameters, susceptibility categories, and related provenance used by the Expert System.
P0130	Normal	PAGASA-Aligned Rainfall Input. The current interface labels for Light, Moderate, Heavy, Intense, and Torrential rainfall and durations of 1, 3, 6, 12, or 24 hours. These selections are hypothetical scenario inputs. Their operational thresholds and use require authoritative source confirmation and validation before official deployment.
P0131	Normal	PostGIS. The spatial extension of PostgreSQL used to store and query FloodSense geographic polygons, points, boundaries, selected locations, and evacuation-center coordinates.
P0132	Normal	Preparedness Provenance. Information identifying the source, validation status, responsible actor, relevant date, and version of a preparedness item or related record. Provenance permits users and administrators to distinguish approved, provisional, and unsupported information.
P0133	Normal	Rainfall Parameters. The supported combination of rainfall intensity and duration selected by the user and applied as scenario facts during Expert System assessment.
P0134	Normal	Rule-Based Expert System. An Expert System in which domain knowledge is represented as conditional rules. FloodSense evaluates applicable rules against known scenario and geographic facts to produce a susceptibility assessment.
P0135	Normal	Web Administration System. The custom browser-based interface used by authorized administrators for dashboards and permission-controlled workflows involving map and rainfall references, parameters, DSS content and structured flows, evacuation centers, data sources, review records, and audit history. It does not expose raw rule editing. Technical Django Admin is separate and is reserved for developer or research maintenance.

P0141	Normal	Related Foreign Literature
P0142	Normal	Flood Susceptibility and Scenario-Based Assessment
P0143	Normal	Flood susceptibility expresses the relative tendency of a location to experience flooding when relevant conditions are present. It differs from flood hazard, which concerns the probability and physical characteristics of an event; exposure, which concerns people or assets in affected places; vulnerability, which concerns the capacity to be harmed; and risk, which combines hazard and potential consequences. Membele, Naidu, and Mutanga (2022) found that flood-vulnerability mapping in developing countries varies substantially in terminology, indicators, spatial scale, data availability, and validation practice. Their review shows why a public system must name the type of assessment it performs and avoid presenting a general vulnerability or hazard map as a locally validated susceptibility conclusion.
P0144	Normal	Scenario-based assessment supports planning by holding selected conditions explicit instead of implying that the system is observing an event as it occurs. A hypothetical rainfall intensity and duration can be treated as assessment inputs only when their meanings, permitted combinations, and geographic applicability are documented. This supports the FloodSense interaction in which the resident selects or confirms a scenario before requesting assessment. It does not establish that the scenario is current, predicted, or officially issued, and it does not justify importing weights or thresholds derived for another place.
P0145	Normal	Deterministic Rule-Based Expert Systems and Knowledge Bases
P0146	Normal	A Rule-Based Expert System represents domain knowledge through facts, conditions, and production rules and applies an inference procedure to derive a conclusion. Buchanan and Shortliffe (1984) provide the foundational distinction between the knowledge base and the inference mechanism. More recently, Papadopoulos et al. (2022) reviewed technologies and standards used in rule-based decision-support systems and described how encoded knowledge and rules engines evaluate case-specific facts. Although their review concerns clinical systems, the architectural distinction is relevant to FloodSense: flood knowledge, scenario facts, geographic facts, and explanations belong to controlled knowledge structures, while the inference procedure determines which eligible rule applies.
P0147	Normal	Deterministic processing means that the same approved facts and the same published rule version should produce the same result. It does not imply that the rules are scientifically valid merely because the software executes them consistently. Knowledge acquisition, conflict handling, parameter definitions, version control, and expert or institutional validation remain necessary. Accordingly, ordinary FloodSense administrators may manage authorized records and permitted values but do not rewrite raw rule conditions, priorities, conflict-resolution logic, or algorithm code.
P0149	Normal	Explainable Inference
P0150	Normal	Explainability is necessary when a system produces a classification that may influence preparedness decisions. Kostopoulos, Davrazos, and Kotsiantis (2024) reviewed explainable decision-support approaches and emphasized transparency, interpretability, and human-understandable justification. Their review primarily addresses artificial-intelligence-based systems, but its communication principles apply to deterministic inference: a user should be able to understand the relevant inputs, the basis of the result, and the limitations of the evidence without being required to inspect source code or raw rule definitions.
P0151	Normal	For FloodSense, an explanation should identify the confirmed rainfall scenario, resolved geographic context, susceptibility class, applicable basis, source or version information, and any limitation state. This supports accountability while preserving the distinction between explanation and rule administration. It also prevents a color or label from appearing authoritative without disclosing what it represents. Explainability does not remove the need for validated data, and a clear explanation cannot turn provisional information into an official local assessment.
P0153	Normal	Decision Support and Preparedness Guidance
P0154	Normal	A Decision Support System organizes information, alternatives, or recommended actions to assist human judgment. In flood preparedness, support may include understandable mitigation choices, household preparation, communication planning, protection of documents and valuables, assistance for vulnerable household members, attention to official advisories, and evacuation readiness. Mostafiz et al. (2022) observed that many web-based flood tools provide technical information but do not consistently provide locally actionable information for individuals and communities. Their review supports combining understandable risk communication with practical guidance while clearly stating the tool's authority and limitations.
P0155	Normal	FloodSense separates the susceptibility assessment from preparedness guidance. The deterministic Expert System produces the assessment, while the DSS presents source-backed pre-event guidance without changing the susceptibility class. Information about evacuation facilities may support readiness when its source, verification date, location, and operational limitations are known, but distance alone does not prove route safety, current opening, accessibility, or available capacity. The DSS therefore assists preparation and does not issue an official warning, evacuation order, or guarantee of safety.
P0156	Normal	GIS, Interactive Maps, and Spatial Databases
P0157	Normal	Geographic Information Systems organize, analyze, and present information associated with locations. Their value in flood research depends on the suitability of the layers, coordinate reference systems, spatial resolution, classification methods, and validation procedures. An administrative boundary can resolve the name of a barangay or provide map context, but it contains no flood-susceptibility fact unless appropriate hazard or susceptibility evidence has been associated with it. This distinction is important when users may interpret any colored polygon as an official hazard layer.
P0158	Normal	Spatial databases support the controlled storage and querying of geographic records. The PostGIS Development Group (2026) explains that PostGIS extends PostgreSQL with geometry and geography types, spatial indexes, spatial relationships, coordinate transformation, validity operations, and spatial processing. These capabilities are relevant to point-in-polygon queries, administrative-area lookup, map-feature storage, and provenance-linked geographic records. They provide technical infrastructure, not scientific validation: a valid geometry and a successful spatial query do not establish that a flood conclusion is institutionally approved.
P0160	Normal	Flood-Risk Communication
P0161	Normal	Flood-risk information must be understandable, actionable, and explicit about uncertainty. Mostafiz et al. (2022) identified gaps in web-based flood communication, particularly where tools provide information without sufficiently connecting it to decisions that residents can make. Clear terms, legends, labels, explanation text, and source notices help prevent non-specialists from treating model outputs as certain predictions. Communication should also distinguish long-term or scenario-based information from time-sensitive official warnings.
P0162	Normal	These principles support a guided FloodSense flow rather than an unexplained technical map. Susceptibility colors must be paired with text, the selected scenario, and limitations. Preparedness information must direct users toward official channels during actual events. The interface must not imply that a neutral boundary layer, a demonstration color, or a provisional record represents a current flood condition.
P0164	Normal	Data Provenance and Geographic Validation
P0165	Normal	Provenance records where information originated, how it was transformed, which version was used, and what review or approval applies. Tullis and Kar (2021) connected provenance to ethical replicability and reproducibility in GIScience, including critical applications in which geographic data affect public decisions. Fakhruddin et al. (2022) similarly emphasized risk-informed data practices and the importance of understanding how disaster and climate data are produced, shared, interpreted, and governed.
P0166	Normal	For FloodSense, provenance should accompany susceptibility parameters, geographic layers, preparedness items, and resource information. Technical geometry validation may confirm coordinate-system consistency, valid shapes, or feature counts, but institutional validation concerns authority, currency, meaning, and permitted public use. The two forms of validation are related but not interchangeable. This distinction prevents a technically processed dataset from being represented as official Bacoor City flood information without documented approval.
P0168	Normal	Foreground Location and Privacy
P0169	Normal	Mobile location can reveal a person's position and may contribute to identification or tracking. Android Developers (2026) distinguishes foreground access, one-time access, and background access and recommends requesting only the permission needed for the active function. This supports a FloodSense flow in which location is obtained only after a user initiates location selection or assessment, while manual selection remains available when the resident declines permission or prefers another area.
P0170	Normal	Foreground acquisition does not imply continuous monitoring. FloodSense does not require background GPS tracking, automatic polling, or autonomous location-based alerts. Location handling should remain purpose-limited to the active assessment, use only the precision necessary for the supported geographic operation, and avoid retaining unnecessary movement information. The presentation must also allow the user to confirm or correct the detected barangay because device accuracy and boundary proximity can affect geographic resolution.
P0172	Normal	Software Testing and Quality Evaluation
P0173	Normal	Software testing establishes whether specified behavior is implemented under defined conditions, while quality evaluation examines broader product characteristics. ISO/IEC 25010:2023 defines a product-quality model with nine characteristics for specifying and evaluating information and communication technology products (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2023). The model can guide the selection of criteria for FloodSense, but it does not provide a ready-made acceptance result or replace an approved evaluation instrument.
P0174	Normal	A defensible product-quality evaluation must identify the tested build, participants or evaluators, tasks, criteria, scale, procedure, valid responses, and interpretation method. Automated tests and interface demonstrations can support technical verification, but they are not user-acceptance findings. The completed initial survey is a needs assessment rather than a post-use system evaluation; therefore, Chapter 2 uses ISO/IEC 25010 as literature supporting the planned product-quality assessment and does not report FloodSense evaluation scores.
P0176	Normal	Existing Foreign Flood-Information Platforms
P0177	Normal	Foreign platforms demonstrate several forms of flood information without constituting transferable evidence for Bacoor City. FloodAdapt uses SFINCS-based scenario simulation and Delft-FIAT impact analysis to compare flood events and adaptation options (Deltares, 2025). The Global Flood Awareness System applies large-scale hydrological modeling to operational river-flood forecasting (Copernicus Emergency Management Service, 2026), while the Iowa Flood Information System combines Iowa-specific observations, radar rainfall, forecasting, and mapped information (Iowa Flood Center, 2024). These services illustrate scenario analysis, forecasting, and public communication, but their models, data, scales, and authority differ from FloodSense.
P0178	Normal	The Resilience Analysis and Planning Tool combines United States hazard, infrastructure, demographic, and community-resilience layers for emergency-management planning (Federal Emergency Management Agency, 2025). England's National Assessment of Flood and Coastal Erosion Risk similarly communicates modeled long-term risk from several flood sources at national scale (Environment Agency, 2024). These official resources show the value of visible limitations, data context, and geographic lookup; however, they are platforms and institutional assessments rather than FloodSense research studies, and their datasets and classifications cannot validate a Bacoor City susceptibility result.

P0187	Normal	Cavite, Bacoor City, and the Imus River Basin
P0188	Normal	The Japan International Cooperation Agency reported the inauguration of flood-mitigation facilities associated with the Imus River Basin and low-lying areas of Imus and Bacoor (JICA, 2021). This institutional record confirms the relevance of watershed and infrastructure conditions to the local context. It does not provide barangay susceptibility classifications, rainfall-rule thresholds, or validation for FloodSense, and it should not be treated as a substitute for hydrologic or hazard data.

Administrative reference and flood-susceptibility evidence must therefore remain separate.
P0191	Normal	Administrative Data Governance and Responsible Use
P0192	Normal	Government publications, local records, and research datasets differ in authority and permitted use. An official list of administrative units may verify names and codes, while an authorized hazard agency may provide hazard information, and a local office may validate operational records. Combining these sources requires documentation of ownership, date, scale, processing, approval, and restrictions. Provenance is particularly important when a dataset has been transformed, because technical processing can change geometry or attributes without changing the institution responsible for the original information.
P0193	Normal	FloodSense consequently distinguishes approved, provisional, technically validated, and unsupported information. The 47-barangay layer may serve as a current administrative reference after documented derivation and geometry checks, but it is not a flood-susceptibility layer and is not automatically City-issued or City-verified. No operational susceptibility dataset should be described as established until the necessary source, method, expert review, institutional authority, and database integration are documented.
P0195	Normal	Philippine Data Privacy and Software-Quality Context
P0196	Normal	Republic Act No. 10173, the Data Privacy Act of 2012, requires personal-information processing to observe transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality. For a resident-facing location function, these principles support clear notice, user initiation, limited collection, secure handling, and avoidance of continuous background tracking. They also support role-controlled administrative access. The Act is legally authoritative despite falling outside the preferred publication period for recent literature.
P0197	Normal	Philippine software studies have also used ISO/IEC 25010 as an evaluation framework, but the criteria and edition must be identified accurately. An evaluation of another application cannot be transferred to FloodSense, and the presence of a questionnaire does not establish completed results. FloodSense quality conclusions will require its own approved instrument, respondents or evaluators, documented procedure, and completed analysis.
P0199	Normal	Philippine Flood-Susceptibility and Risk Mapping
P0200	Normal	Puno et al. (2022) used GIS frequency-ratio analysis, 160 flashflood inventory points, and nine conditioning factors to map susceptibility in Barangay Bukang Liwayway, Bukidnon. Gacu et al. (2022) combined hazard, vulnerability, and exposure parameters through a GIS-based analytical hierarchy process in Odiongan, Romblon, using judgments from ten experts and end-users and checks against ground assessment and historical events. These studies demonstrate documented local spatial methods and validation practices, but they are mapping investigations rather than resident-facing flood-information systems.
P0201	Normal	Their factors, weights, classifications, geographic scales, and reported results are specific to their study areas. They support the need for local data and validation but do not provide Bacoor City rules or an operational susceptibility dataset. Accordingly, they are retained as Philippine literature relevant to spatial assessment and are not presented in the system-study comparison tables.
P0202	Normal	Existing Philippine Hazard-Information and Planning Platforms
P0203	Normal	UP NOAH remains an important Philippine reference for accessible geographic hazard information. Lagmay et al. (2017) documented the dissemination of near-real-time hazard information and flood maps through Web-GIS using open-source code and application programming interfaces. The dated publication supports discussion of public map-based risk communication, while the general platform should not be treated as a research study with an invented SDLC. UP NOAH differs from FloodSense because its documented hazard-information resources were institutionally developed, whereas FloodSense is a scenario-based research prototype and is not an official forecasting or warning platform.
P0204	Normal	HazardHunterPH is a GeoRisk Philippines application that produces site-specific indicative multi-hazard assessments from official agency information (GeoRisk Philippines, 2023). Its rapid geographic lookup and source-based reporting are relevant to FloodSense's emphasis on accessible information, provenance, and visible limitations. It remains an official platform rather than a substitute for locally validated FloodSense rules or data, and its reports do not confer forecasting or warning authority on FloodSense.
P0205	Normal	GeoMapperPH supports governed collection of hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and coping-capacity information by trained government and agency users, while PlanSmartPH Ready to Rebuild organizes information for local-government rehabilitation and recovery planning (DOST-PHIVOLCS, 2022a, 2022b). These platforms illustrate structured data governance and institutional workflows. They are discussed as existing Philippine systems, not retained as Related Studies, because the inspected official pages do not provide a complete research methodology and results section suitable for the comparative study tables.
P0206	Normal	Related Foreign Studies
P0207	Normal	Design and Implementation of Real-Time Flood Early Warning System (FEWS) Based on IoT Blynk Application: An Arduino- and GSM-Based Mobile Flood-Warning System
P0208	Normal	Wisudawan (2021) designed and implemented a real-time Flood Early Warning System at a reservoir of Universitas Islam Indonesia. The system combined Arduino-based rain, temperature, humidity, and water-level sensing with GSM communication and the Blynk Android application. Its field implementation recorded observations for 30 days and classified readings as Normal, Advisory, Watch, or Warning.
P0209	Normal	The reported mean temperature, humidity, and water level were 28.6 degrees Celsius, 63.7%, and 54.5 centimeters, and no flood occurred during the observation period. The system demonstrates sensor-to-mobile warning delivery, but its single-site, short-duration test did not include an actual flood event. Unlike FloodSense, it performs continuous environmental monitoring and warning rather than a resident-requested hypothetical susceptibility assessment, and its thresholds cannot be transferred to Bacoor City.
P0210	Normal	A User Experience Evaluation of a Mobile Application for Disseminating Site-Specific Impact-Based Flood Warnings: The A4alerts App: A Mobile Application for Site-Specific Impact-Based Flood Warnings
P0211	Normal	Meléndez-Landaverde and Sempere-Torres (2023) evaluated A4alerts, a mobile crisis application linked to an operational site-specific flood early-warning system in Catalonia, Spain. Seventy-six participants from civil-protection, emergency-response, and local stakeholder groups completed an online interactive experiment from January to March 2022 using realistic flood scenarios. The study measured usefulness, functional appropriateness, and adoption and compared feature rankings through Friedman's test and Bonferroni-adjusted Wilcoxon tests.
P0212	Normal	Ninety-seven percent considered the application useful during flood emergencies, and 93% stated that they would use or recommend it. Site-specific impact warnings, official regional warnings, and a self-protection checklist were ranked most useful. The evaluation was confined to recruited Catalan stakeholder networks, an online demonstration, and perceived usefulness rather than actual behavior during an event. A4alerts supports FloodSense's emphasis on location context and understandable guidance, but it disseminates operational warnings and does not validate FloodSense for Bacoor City.
P0213	Normal	Real-Time Flood Forecasting and Warning: A Comprehensive Approach toward HCI-Centric Mobile App Development: A Human-Centered Mobile Flood-Forecasting and Warning Prototype
P0214	Normal	Alsabhan and Dudin (2023) developed an HCI-centered framework and functional prototype for mobile real-time flood forecasting and warning. Activity theory and scenario-based design guided a nominal-group process involving five prospective users and five experts, who generated 63 situations; 12 priority scenarios were retained for requirements analysis. Prototype evaluation examined task completion and satisfaction involving usefulness, ease of use, learnability, satisfaction, and overall experience.
P0215	Normal	The process produced 93 design requirements and identified front-end users, weather-station users, and administrators. Reported mean satisfaction-category scores ranged from 3.93 to 4.08, while some analytical tasks remained more difficult. The study provides evidence for role-specific access and user-centered flood interfaces, but its small participant group and specific forecasting architecture limit generalization. FloodSense shares the concern for understandable interfaces and permissions but does not provide real-time forecasting or warnings.
P0216	Normal	Developing a Climate-Smart Web GIS App for Multi-Hazard Early Warning against Climate-Based Disaster Risks: A Tanzanian Web GIS for Flood and Drought Early Warning
P0217	Normal	Msusa et al. (2025) developed a Web GIS application for flood and drought early warning in Tanzania. The study used Height Above Nearest Drainage analysis and GloFAS forecast data for flooding in Dar es Salaam, a ConvLSTM model and geospatial weather data for drought in Dodoma, participatory mapping with vulnerable communities for flood validation, and Rapid Application Development for the application.
P0218	Normal	Community-mapped flood points showed strong spatial agreement with the flood-model output, while the drought model reported a median ROC-AUC of 0.87 and median F1 score of 0.81. The Web GIS integrated the resulting information for dissemination. Its dynamic forecast inputs, multi-hazard models, Tanzanian setting, and RAD process differ from FloodSense's deterministic hypothetical-scenario assessment. Its model performance and mapped areas cannot be generalized to Bacoor City.
P0219	Normal	Related Local Studies
P0220	Normal	Web-Based Solution for Flood Warning Decision Support in the Province of Leyte, Philippines: An IoT Rainfall- and Water-Level Decision-Support Prototype
P0221	Normal	Bentoso et al. (2021) developed an automated flood-warning prototype for the Province of Leyte using rainfall and water-level sensors, data transmission, a web interface, graphical reporting, threshold-based alerts, and decision-support outputs. The documented technical procedure focused on acquiring environmental readings, converting them into understandable web reports, and delivering alert messages to responsible authorities.
P0222	Normal	The authors reported that the system displayed the flooding situation graphically, delivered alert-level messages, and supported monitoring and flood-control planning. The publication establishes a functional research prototype but does not establish province-wide deployment or transferability of its sensor thresholds. It is relevant to digital decision support, but FloodSense does not conduct continuous sensor monitoring or issue alerts, and Leyte-specific procedures cannot validate a Bacoor assessment.
P0223	Normal	Isabela City Flood Monitoring System Using Arduino and GSM Module: An Arduino and GSM Flood-Monitoring and SMS-Warning System
P0224	Normal	Carpio, Asnaul, Said, and Amerin (2022) developed an Arduino Uno and GSM flood-monitoring system for Riverside Barangay in Isabela City, Basilan. The authors expressly used an Agile Development Model for incremental system development and a quantitative survey design for evaluation. Twenty City Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Office personnel participated in the system demonstration and initial device test covering functionality, design, security, efficiency, and reliability.
P0225	Normal	The study reported an overall mean of 3.70, interpreted by the authors as Acceptable, and described the device as capable of providing warning signals under the tested conditions. It also identified a battery operating limit of approximately ten hours. The system shows a Philippine implementation of sensor monitoring and SMS warning, but its hardware, respondents, local thresholds, and evaluation cannot be transferred to Bacoor City. FloodSense instead uses a user-confirmed scenario and does not issue automatic warnings.
P0226	Normal	IOT-Based Flood Alert System for Narvacan, Sto. Tomas, La Union: A Water-Level Monitoring, Web, and SMS Alert Prototype
P0227	Normal	Dela Cruz et al. (2024) used descriptive and developmental research designs and Agile methodology to develop an IoT water-level monitoring and alert prototype for Barangay Narvacan, Sto. Tomas, La Union. Interviews and observations informed development, while purposive sampling selected 30 evaluators: nine barangay council officials and 21 residents from seven sectors. The User Technology Acceptability Questionnaire assessed usefulness, information quality, and interface quality after system testing.
P0228	Normal	The system obtained a grand mean of 6.87 on the study's seven-point scale, interpreted as extremely high acceptance; reported category means included 6.87 for usefulness, 6.83 for information quality, and 6.82 for interface quality. The results apply to the selected community and prototype, not to Bacoor City. Its continuous sensor readings, web monitoring, and SMS alerts differ from FloodSense's pre-event scenario assessment and separate preparedness guidance.
P0229	Normal	BantayBaha: Flood Monitoring System with SMS Notification: An IoT Water-Level Monitoring, Web-Logging, and SMS Notification System
P0230	Normal	Delos Reyes, Cabreros, Paulino, and Garcia (2020) developed BantayBaha for Barangay Virgen Delas Flores in Baliwag, Bulacan after examining the barangay's manual measurement and megaphone-based warning practice. The project used IoT water-level sensing, SMS notification, and website logging, followed by verification, validation, repeated testing, and an evaluation involving 70 Baliuag University students.
P0231	Normal	The respondents rated system functionality at 4.70, user interface at 4.50, and reliability at 4.57, each interpreted by the authors as Very Satisfactory. The study was limited to a single barangay and identified further hardware improvements, including alternative power and additional sensing. BantayBaha is relevant as a local digital warning system, but its evaluators, sensor conditions, and alert functions do not establish Bacoor-specific susceptibility or FloodSense effectiveness.
P0232	Normal	Synthesis of the Reviewed Literature
P0233	Normal	The reviewed literature and systems demonstrate several established approaches to flood information: sensor-based monitoring, forecast dissemination, Web GIS, mobile warnings, explainable interfaces, preparedness checklists, and Philippine SMS alert prototypes. The foreign systems show that usefulness depends on clear location context, role-appropriate interfaces, understandable warnings, and evaluation with users or geographic data. The Philippine systems show that local implementations commonly combine sensors, web or mobile presentation, and alert messages, although their thresholds, hardware, samples, and findings remain tied to their respective communities.
P0234	Normal	These systems agree that flood information must be understandable and connected to action, but they differ in purpose, authority, data, method, geographic scale, and disaster-management phase. Wisudawan (2021), Bentoso et al. (2021), Carpio et al. (2022), Dela Cruz et al. (2024), and Delos Reyes et al. (2020) rely on sensed water or rainfall conditions. A4alerts disseminates warnings from an operational warning system, the HCI-centered prototype addresses real-time forecasting, and the Tanzanian Web GIS integrates forecast and predictive models. FloodSense does not adopt these monitoring, forecasting, or automatic-warning functions.
P0235	Normal	The literature on susceptibility, GIS, deterministic rules, decision support, provenance, privacy, and software quality supports the verified FloodSense design. FloodSense combines a resident-confirmed hypothetical rainfall scenario and location with deterministic rule matching, an explained susceptibility output, and preparedness guidance that remains separate from assessment. Authentication, roles, source records, review status, and administrative controls support responsible use. These design choices do not eliminate the need for locally appropriate knowledge, geographic validation, expert review, and institutional authorization.
P0236	Normal	The methods and results of the reviewed systems cannot be transferred directly to Bacoor City. Sensor thresholds, hydrological models, participant ratings, map classes, forecast inputs, and reported accuracy were produced in different environments and for different users. Their contributions therefore guide concepts and methods rather than supplying Bacoor-specific susceptibility facts or proving that FloodSense is effective.
P0237	Normal	Research Gap
P0238	Normal	Existing studies already provide evaluated flood-monitoring devices, mobile warning applications, Web GIS platforms, and decision-support prototypes. What remains insufficiently addressed in the inspected evidence is a Bacoor-focused, public-facing flow in which a resident deliberately selects a hypothetical rainfall scenario and location, receives an explainable deterministic susceptibility result, and then accesses preparedness guidance as a separate form of support.
P0239	Normal	The gap also concerns provenance and local validity. None of the reviewed systems supplies an approved Bacoor City susceptibility knowledge base, locally validated rule thresholds, authorized geographic inputs, or evidence that external results can be generalized to the city's 47 barangays. FloodSense addresses the combination of scenario input, explainable rule-based assessment, location confirmation, separate preparedness support, authentication, and administrative governance as a research system. It remains distinct from official forecasting, warning, monitoring, and emergency-command systems.

P0258	Normal	METHODOLOGY
P0259	Normal	This chapter presents the materials and methods used to identify resident and institutional requirements, develop FloodSense, analyze the initial needs-assessment survey, verify the software, and prepare the product-quality evaluation.
P0260	Normal	Materials
P0261	Normal	Research materials included the bilingual resident questionnaire and consent notice, Google Forms and the exported response dataset, interview guides and recorded institutional interviews, request letters and approved school documents, scholarly and official references, the development-manuscript alignment records, requirements documents, diagrams, interface designs, test cases, and verification records.
P0262	Normal	System-development materials included Windows computers, Android test devices, Git version control, Flutter and Dart for the resident application, Python with Django and Django REST Framework for the backend and administration system, PostgreSQL/PostGIS for relational and spatial storage, Mapbox and OpenStreetMap-compatible mapping components, and browser-based administration interfaces. These materials supported development and testing; their presence did not by itself establish the scientific or institutional validity of geographic or susceptibility information.
P0263	Normal	Method
P0264	Normal	The study used a developmental-descriptive research design. The developmental component covered requirements analysis, design, iterative construction, integration, and technical verification of the resident application, backend services, geographic functions, deterministic Expert System, Decision Support System, and administration system. The descriptive component summarized the experiences, information problems, preparedness needs, and technology conditions reported in the initial resident survey. Institutional interviews and document review supplied qualitative evidence concerning current practices, data ownership, provenance, validation, and operational boundaries.
P0265	Normal	FloodSense followed an Agile Development Method. This method was selected because requirements, interfaces, backend services, geographic functions, and tests were refined through repeated cycles of planning, design, implementation, verification, review, and correction. The study does not claim Scrum or Kanban ceremonies that are not established by the project records. Agile activities were organized into the following recurring phases:
P0266	Normal	• Requirements and planning. Resident survey evidence, institutional interviews, documents, verified literature, and system constraints were translated into traceable resident and administrator requirements.
P0267	Normal	• Design. The researchers prepared functional modules, interfaces, geographic flows, data structures, permissions, and test conditions before or alongside implementation.
P0268	Normal	• Iterative development. Mobile, backend, geographic, Expert System, Decision Support System, account, and administrative functions were implemented and integrated in manageable increments.
P0269	Normal	• Testing and review. Automated and manual checks were used to compare actual behavior with defined requirements, identify defects or contradictions, and return necessary corrections to the next development cycle.
P0270	Normal	• Release preparation. Stable functions, source and validation boundaries, privacy controls, and documentation were reviewed before a build could proceed to formal user evaluation or deployment preparation.
P0271	Normal	Requirements Gathering
P0272	Normal	Resident requirements were gathered through the initial questionnaire on flood experience, information sources, information problems, effects on decisions, preparedness needs, evacuation-center information, Android access, connectivity, language preference, and location-sharing comfort. BDRRMO and CPDCO interviews and documents were examined for institutional practices, data ownership, geographic records, preparedness procedures, evacuation information, and validation restrictions. Literature and official publications were used to define concepts and safety boundaries.
P0273	Normal	Survey and interview evidence was not converted directly into a feature list. Each candidate requirement was checked for relevance to the research questions, consistency with the established pre-event scope, technical feasibility, privacy and safety implications, source authority, and compatibility with the current system architecture. Requests for capabilities outside the approved scope were retained as research context rather than represented as FloodSense functions.
P0274	Normal	Participants and Sampling
P0275	Normal	Purposive sampling was used for the initial needs assessment. The final dataset contained 40 qualified respondents who consented, were at least 18 years old, lived in Bacoor City, and had experienced flooding or serious heavy-rain effects. Respondents came from 14 of the city's 47 barangays. Niog contributed 13 respondents, Zapote I to III contributed 15, and ten other barangays contributed 12. Because 70.0% of the sample came from Niog and Zapote I to III, the results describe the participating respondents and are not generalized to the entire Bacoor City population. A conventional response rate was not computed because no fixed sampling frame or distribution denominator was established.
P0276	Normal	Thirty respondents (75.0%) were 18 to 34 years old. Key informants from BDRRMO and CPDCO were included for institutional requirements and data-governance context. Their statements were used to explain practices, sources, and limitations rather than as evidence of resident acceptance or system effectiveness.

P0278	Normal	Research Instruments
P0279	Normal	The primary needs-assessment instrument was the bilingual FloodSense Resident Survey Questionnaire. It contained a Data Privacy Act notice and consent item, respondent eligibility and demographic questions, flood-experience items, current-information and preparedness items, evacuation-center information needs, Android and internet access, location-sharing preferences, and optional comments. Institutional interview guides and document requests were used for BDRRMO and CPDCO evidence.
P0280	Normal	Technical verification used unit, integration, API, spatial, permission, and end-to-end test cases. The planned product-quality evaluation will use an approved instrument based on relevant ISO/IEC 25010:2023 characteristics. The initial resident questionnaire is a needs-assessment instrument and is not treated as a post-use evaluation of FloodSense.
P0281	Normal	Data Gathering Procedure
P0282	Normal	The initial survey was distributed to eligible Bacoor City residents and closed on October 11, 2026. Responses were exported, screened for consent and eligibility, normalized for barangay reporting, and summarized without direct personal identifiers. The screening retained all 40 qualified submissions while documenting quality concerns, including one identical response pair, five unusually fast submissions, repeated long comments in six records, and some mixed selections in items that were intended to be mutually exclusive. These records were not silently removed because the available evidence did not establish that they were invalid; the limitations are disclosed when interpreting the results.
P0283	Normal	Institutional evidence was collected through interviews, written requests, and review of available records. Research sources were checked against their primary publications or official documentation. Development requirements and test conditions were then revised through successive Agile cycles, with contradictions resolved according to source authority, research scope, safety, privacy, and implementation evidence.
P0284	Normal	Statistical Treatment and Qualitative Analysis
P0285	Normal	Survey responses were analyzed using frequencies and percentages for categorical and multiple-response items. Ordered five-point items were summarized through their distributions and arithmetic means using the scoring defined in the approved survey summary. The eight information-problem items all fell within the survey's Often interpretation; the highest means concerned information that was too general for the respondent's location (4.16), difficulty accessing information when internet service or electricity was unreliable (4.14), preparedness advice that was too general (4.11), and unclear information source or date (4.08).
P0286	Normal	Open comments were coded thematically for recurring information needs, map or location concerns, preparedness, warnings, severity, and evacuation-center information. Themes were interpreted as needs-assessment evidence and were not treated as proof that a requested capability belonged within the approved FloodSense scope. Institutional interviews and documents were coded for current practices, data ownership, provenance, validation, permissions, and system boundaries.
P0287	Normal	System Development and Technical Verification
P0288	Normal	The resident application was developed in Flutter, while the backend, REST API, geographic services, and custom Web Administration System were developed with Django, Django REST Framework, GeoDjango, and PostgreSQL/PostGIS. The assessment service applies deterministic rule matching and conflict handling to the confirmed scenario and geographic facts. Preparedness guidance is delivered through a separate structured Decision Support System and does not alter the susceptibility class.
P0289	Normal	Technical verification covers authentication and permissions, account flows, scenario and location confirmation, input validation, rule matching and conflict handling, API responses, spatial queries, map behavior, preparedness flows, evacuation-center lookup, administrative workflows, and mobile-to-service integration. Test records identify the precondition, input, expected result, actual result, status, environment, and defect reference where applicable. Technical test success establishes behavior under the tested conditions but does not establish scientific validity, institutional approval, usability, or production readiness.

Automated technical results and user evaluation results will be reported separately because they answer different questions.
