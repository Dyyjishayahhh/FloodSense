Abstract
People-centred early warning systems must ensure that their warnings are
timely, actionable and get to the largest number of exposed individuals in the
shortest time possible. Due to the increased access to high-bandwidth commu-
nication, mobile applications have become a valuable complement to tradi-
tional sources of emergency information. However, there is limited research
on how useful end-users perceive these apps and their functionalities in the
context of flood emergencies. This research presents the first online user expe-
rience evaluation of the A4alerts, a crisis app developed to disseminate the
flood warnings triggered by the operational impact-based early warning system
in Catalonia, Spain. Following an interactive experiment using the A4alerts
app to access emergency information within real-based flood scenarios, partici-
pants were prompted to measure their experience using statements linked to
criteria on usefulness, functional appropriateness, and adoption. The evalua-
tion results suggest that sampled participants considered the A4alerts a useful
communication tool to support end-users and their emergency actions. In par-
ticular, the local impact-based flood warnings, regional official warnings and
self-protection action checklist were ranked as the most useful functionalities
implemented within the app. These findings justify future iterations of the
A4alerts for supporting the rapid dissemination of emergency information and
helping communities reduce the impacts of flood events.

emergency management, flash flood, forecasting and warning, risk communication

Flash floods result from extreme precipitation in an area
exceeding the natural or drainage system capacities
(Seneviratne et al.,
2021). Typically, these affect small to
medium size catchments characterised by steep
topography, thus, prone to convective storms (Corral
et al., 2019; Ritter et al., 2020). As seen during the 2021
floods in Belgium, Germany, and The Netherlands, flash
floods can have devastating negative socio-economic
impacts in communities, including grave danger to
human lives and economic damages in the range of

Regardless of warnings issued in advance by the national
meteorological and hydrological services for the 2021
floods, the message on the potential impacts and mitiga-
tion actions failed to reach some of the most vulnerable
communities (Fekete & Sandholz,
2021). These previous
events have exposed the significant gap between the
impact forecasting tools available and the timely warnings
communities need to effectively mitigate the negative con-
sequences of floods. Alarmingly, these types of extreme
floods are only becoming more frequent, with most of the
recorded events from 1860 and 2016 in Europe categorised
as flash floods (Bednar-Friedl et al.,
2022). Hence, due to
their explosive and rapidly evolving nature (usually not
more than a few hours), integrating response and effective
risk communication mechanisms to support communities
during emergencies is essential and a major challenge
Aligned with target G of the Sendai Framework for Disas-
ter Risk Reduction, end-to-end early warning systems
(EWS) must incorporate the following four interrelated
pillars: (i) disaster risk knowledge; (ii) detection, monitor-
ing, analysis, and forecasting; (iii) dissemination and com-
munication; and (iv) preparedness at all levels. By
integrating the above key elements, EWS can increase the
likelihood of issuing warnings that are understandable,
actionable and get to the largest number of exposed indi-
viduals in the shortest time possible (UNISDR,
2015).
Communities expect hazard and local impact information
to be readily available and accessible, especially when the
time window for action during flash flood emergencies is
limited (Dallo & Marti,
2021).
Information and communication technologies (ICT)
have become promising tools to support the rapid dissem-
ination of flood warnings; process and share real-time
emergency information; and establish communication
channels between authorities, responders and citizens
during evolving flood emergencies (Grinko et al.,
2019).
While the traditional methods of dissemination (e.g., TV
news and radio) remain essential sources of information,
the increasing number of people using wireless technolo-
gies with accessible internet connection allows the explo-
ration of more flexible and innovative methods for flood
risk communication (Stanciugelu et al.,
2017). Within
crisis-related ICT, mobile hazard crisis apps started to be
officially implemented in parallel to help facilitate the
swift distribution of context-specific messages (Reuter,
Kaufhold, & Knipp,
2017; Reuter, Kaufhold, Spielhofer, &
Hahne, 2017). In this regard, the promoted frameworks
by the World Meteorological Organization for improving
the response of communities during flood emergencies,
such as local impact-based EWS, can benefit from these
advantages hazard crisis apps can offer (Stanciugelu
et al.,
2017; WMO, 2021).
As part of the recently developed and implemented
community-based“site-specific EWS” (SS-EWS) (Meléndez-
Landaverde & Sempere-Torres, 2022), a flood crisis mobile
application named“A4alerts” has been designed to dissemi-
nate the location-specific flood warnings and associated
self-protection actions trig gered by an operative impact-
based EWS. In this context, the A4alerts was developed to
support the rapid flood risk communication and help
enable appropriate actions to decrease the negative impacts
at vulnerable points or community areas. This work pre-
sents the A4alerts app, its functionalities and the first
online user experience evaluation performed in Catalonia,
Spain.
The paper is structured as follows: Section
2 provides
background on the previous work regarding crisis mobile
apps for emergency communication. Section
3 presents
the A4alerts, its functionalities and the operational EWS
behind the app. Section 4 describes the experimental
online demo developed for evaluating the A4alerts from
an end-user perspective within the context of flood emer-
gencies. Section 5 deals with the results and their impli-
cations. Section 6 describes the encountered limitations
and areas for future research. Finally, some concluding
remarks are presented in Section 7.

An essential emergency feature of any effective flood
EWS is the rapid dissemination of relevant information
and behaviour advice to a broad audience of exposed
and vulnerable individuals. As part of the four key ele-
ments of end-to-end EWSs, the dissemination and com-
munication of warnings in real time are essential to
enable timely and appropriate mitigation and self-
protection actions to help r educe the negative impacts
of flood events (WMO,
2018). In light of this, various

used during emergencies. In the United States, the

in general, well-perceived by the group of participants,

Until now, the general development of hazard crisis
apps and flood-specific technological platforms has
focused on the technical capabilities and their implica-
tions regarding real-time implementation (Colombelli
et al.,
2020; Dallo & Marti, 2021). However, recognising
the importance of employing people-centred methodolo-
gies, the work on hazard crisis apps is shifting from the
traditional technocratic perspective. In this context,
the latest research has focused on exploring how their
design and individual features can effectively support the
decision-making process of end-users to increase
the probability of continuous usage and social integration
(Paul et al.,
2021). The usefulness of hazard crisis apps
during emergencies from the point of view of potential
end-users; the functional appropriateness of their fea-
tures to support their decision-making process; and the
technical implications on learnability and social adoption
are elements that have been explored in recent years
(Dallo & Marti,
2021; Fischer-Preßler et al., 2021). As
recognised by the participants in the study performed by
Tan et al. (
2020b), end-users will not install or keep using
a hazard crisis app without any apparent added value
towards usefulness and appropriate support during emer-
gencies. In this regard, previous studies have proposed
guidelines and recommendations for designing general
hazard crisis apps and their functionalities based on the
expectations and expressed needs of participants through
workshops and guided discussions. However, despite
these existing studies, there is limited research on how
their results can be applied explicitly to the design of cri-
sis app prototypes for hydrometeorological hazards.
Moreover, there is a larger significant knowledge gap
regarding the specific functionalities end-users require to
effectively support their preparedness and mitigation
actions during weather-induced emergencies. Additional
studies are necessary to identify the requirements of com-
munities, authorities, and responders regarding innova-
tive communication technologies to justify the
functionalities that should be incorporated in crisis apps
in the context of flood events.
Finally, these apps have inherent obstacles to wider
acceptance, adoption, and usage. The long-term effectiveness
of hazard crisis apps as a communication channel during
emergencies is strongly influenced by the number of active
users, as seen in the recent COVID-19 pandemic (Trang
et al.,
2020). Although the popularity and public knowl-
edge of hazard crisis apps have grown, improving the level
of integration of these specialised apps in society and
maintaining a stable user base are some of the main chal-
lenges ICT and authorities currently face (Fischer-Preßler
et al.,
2021). Moreover, vulnerable groups such as individ-
uals with disabilities (e.g., visual or hearing impairments)
are at risk of being excluded or neglected when consider-
ing the design and technical requirements of hazard crisis
apps (Paul et al.,
2021). Interestingly, though the elderly
have been categorised as technological-averse, the recent
study by Appleby-Arnold et al. ( 2019) suggests these
groups can have a positive attitude towards using hazard
crisis apps to support their actions during emergencies.
There is a demand for more focused and context-specific
research on the factors influencing the acceptance and
intention to use crisis apps during weather-induced
events.
3 | THE A4ALERTS MOBILE
APPLICATION
From 2020 to mid-2021, the A4alerts app was designed,
developed and internally tested alongside a group of stake-
holders (n = 15) integrated by the responsible civil protec-
tion authorities, first responders and local experts in
disaster risk management of Terrassa, Spain, through the
iterative community-based SS-EWS framework (Meléndez-
Landaverde & Sempere-Torres,
2022). Figure 1a presents
the six sections of the current A4alerts app: (1) main or
home view; (2) site-specific warnings (SSWs); (3) report an
event; (4) feedback; (5) official warnings; and (6) informa-
tion. The description of the warning system behind the
app, the functionalities and how the app works during
flood emergencies are presented below.

community-based SS-EWS framework

meteorology of the Polytechnic University of Catalonia
within the H2020 ANYWHERE project, 1 the SS-EWS
framework guides communities to co-design, implement
and co-evaluate impact-based flood warnings (i.e., SSWs)
alongside self-protection actions for specific vulnerable
points (e.g., sites in a flood zone area) or community

3.2 | A4alerts functionalities
Table 1 shows the 10 functionalities implemented in the
current A4alerts prototype version. These have been
selected based on the recent studies on the needs and
requirements of end-users for the development of hazard
crisis apps (Dallo & Marti,
2021; Tan et al., 2020a). In this
regard, the A4alerts functionalities have the objective of
(i) informing about the active site-specific and official
rainfall warnings; (ii) proposing actions (per warning
level) from official self-protection plans to mitigate local
impacts; (iii) helping enhance flood risk understanding;
and (iv) enabling the participation of end-users to sup-
port authorities, emergency responders and citizens.
Due to the complexity of the required validation,
quality control and privacy measures for their widespread
public implementation (Imran et al.,
2015; Reuter &
Kaufhold, 2018), the “report and event ” and “photo vali-
dation” functionalities have been included in the A4alerts
app only as prototypes to exemplify their usage. This
decision was made to concentrate the initial efforts on
exploring and understanding the uptake of these func-
tionalities by the testing group and the participants of the
online demo within the context of a flood scenario. Thus,
these sections can always be accessed from the main
screen. In the report an event section, end-users can
notify about an incident they have observed by uploading
an image and a short description to improve the under-
standing of the situation or facilitate its location. After
manually validating them through the back-end system,
these images are shown on the background map of the
main screen for other testing users to see and verify.
Finally, since the A4alerts is currently in constant revi-
sion, a feedback section has been introduced to allow par-
ticipants to send suggestions or report any problems they
have experienced while using the app.

users can access the “SSW section” (Figure
1b), consisting
of the co-designed impact-based flood warning text for the
site, pictures of relevant historical flood events in the area,
the self-protection actions checklist, and the warning

4 | A4ALERTS USER EXPERIENCE
EVALUATION
This study aims to evaluate the current version of the
A4alerts app from an end-user perspective within
the context of flood emergencies. Similar to previous
studies (Meléndez-Landaverde et al.,
2020; Terti
et al., 2019), an experimental role-playing demo in an
online environment was prepared to showcase the func-
tionalities of the A4alerts app during a previous real-case
flood scenario. For this purpose, the online demo was
pre-tested by six members of the stakeholder group from
Terrassa (see Section
3) and finalised based on their feed-
back. The last version of the demo was disseminated
online via email within the internal communication net-
work of first responders in Catalonia. The participants
recruited on a voluntary (anonymous) basis included
members of civil protection, emergency responders and
local stakeholder groups of Terrassa and Catalonia.
4.1 | Materials: Online
experimental demo
The five sections of the online demo are presented in
Table 2. The first section consisted of general instructions
and an introductory video to help explain the A4alerts
app and its functionalities to the participants. Section 2
contained the flood scenario alongside their triggered
SSWs delivered through a web version of the A4alerts
app. As in previous studies (Meléndez-Landaverde
et al.,
2020; Potter et al., 2018), the information presented
in the flood scenario introduced participants to their
assigned role, its responsibilities, and their decision-
making obligations in case specific protection actions
should be needed due to the active SSWs. In this regard,
the warnings were provided within an interactive web
version of the A4alerts app. This web version was sepa-
rately prepared and embedded inside the experimental
demo so participants could explore and understand how
the app and its functionalities (see Table
1) would work
during a flood scenario. Accordingly, the rainfall episode
used in this study was based on the 16th of August high-
impact event experienced in 2020 at Terrassa. Although
short in duration (2 h), it caused surface water accumula-
tions across Terrassa, flooding of underground parking,
fallen trees and notable rescue activities by first responders
(Amores et al.,
2020; Consejo de Ministros, 2020). The SS-
EWS was run with the corrected hydrometeorological data
to produce the warnings presented in the web version of
the A4alerts app.

essential to uncover the functionalities perceived as the
most useful in the context of flood emergencies to (i) help

4.2 | Procedure
To increase the probability of capturing a significant
number of responses and due to the ongoing COVID-19
restrictions, the user experience evaluation was conceived
in an online environment. In this context, the survey
application Limesurvey was used to code and publish the
experimental demo online and to collect the responses.
After reading the initial instructions and viewing a video
of the A4alerts app, participants were exposed to the
flood scenario. In this section, participants had to use
the web version of the A4alerts app to access the latest
SSWs, local behavioural guidelines and the other avail-
able emergency functionalities to decide if self-protection
actions were needed based on the provided information.
At the end of the flood scenario, participants were
directed to the post-evaluation to evaluate the A4alerts
app and its functionalities based exclusively on their pre-
vious experience. Finally, general questions to identify
the demographic characteristics of participants were
asked as a last step of the online demo.

evaluation
Figure 3 shows the percentage of agreement with the
statements regarding the A4alerts app and its functionali-
ties presented in Table

below the “disagree” level are considered negative evalu-
ations; equal and above the “agree” level as positive eval-
uations; and “neutral” is a state of indecision or lack of
convincing in participants and, thus, analysed by the
authors in the context of the total percentage for each
answer level. Most respondents positively evaluated the
A4alerts app and its functionalities for most of the state-
ments linked to the ANYWHERE co-evaluation criteria.
In this context, many participants considered that the
current version of the app is well integrated, useful, and
has functionalities that are understandable enough to
support the protection actions that might be needed dur-
ing flood emergencies.
For the overall A4alerts app evaluation, a significant
percentage of the participants agreed that it could be a
useful tool during flood emergencies (97%) and that they
would use or recommend the app to a friend or family
member (93%). However, even though the interface of
the app was considered intuitive (87%) and aesthetically
pleasing (75%), a large percentage of respondents were
not confident that someone could use the app without
prior training or first external guidance (87% equal or
below neutral level). As mentioned at the beginning of
Section

demo included active members within the first and sec-
ond responder groups and official authorities in Terrassa
and Catalonia. Although the app was considered useful
to assist these groups during flood emergencies (96%), the
results suggest that to successfully use the app and its
functionalities, resources (e.g., time, personnel, and
material) should be allocated to provide training or demo
sessions. Non-surprisingly, previous research has found

Hazard and specialised crisis apps such as the A4alerts
have specific design characteristics (e.g., warning colour
codes and symbols); functionalities (e.g., radar maps,
reporting and validation of impacts); and usability con-

to appropriately train end-users on the specialised flood

Regarding the functional appropriateness of the
A4alerts features, many participants agreed that the indi-
vidual functionalities were easy to understand (78%) and
appropriately combined to support emergency actions
during flood events (81%). As mentioned in Section
3, the
functionalities incorporated in the A4alerts app follow
the recommendations of recent qualitative research on
the requirements and the preferences in features for
disaster and hazard crisis apps by potential end-users
(Dallo & Marti,
2021; Reuter, Kaufhold, & Knipp, 2017;
Reuter, Kaufhold, Spielhofer, & Hahne, 2017). In this
context, integrating functionalities and information
desired by target end-users can help these technological
emergency tools and services to be perceived as useful
and, thus, increase the probability of adoption
(i.e., installation and continuous use) (Fischer-Preßler
et al.,
2021; Grinko et al., 2019). The quantitative results
of this study indicate that A4alerts app functionalities
evaluated in the demo are positively perceived by partici-
pants to support their actions during flood emergencies.
Finally, due to growing research on innovative func-
tionalities to support the contribution of end-users and
enable their communication with emergency responders
(Finazzi,
2020;K a u f h o l de ta l . ,2018), the willingness to
send geo-located photos to report impacts was individually
evaluated. In this study and as suggested by previous
research (Appleby-Arnold et al., 2019), most participants
agreed they would send geo-located impact photos
through the app (67%). These features have been found to
help enhance the situational awareness of authorities and
citizens and enrich the local real-time information for
emergency actions and future impact validations, a critical
aspect of impact-based EWSs (Lara Tan et al.,
2017;
McCallum et al., 2016). However, their successful real-
time implementation has some challenges. Data privacy
and protection concerns can hinder the disposition of end-
users to trust and share the personal and GPS information
needed by these functionalities (Fischer et al., 2019;Y a b e
et al., 2022). Furthermore, awareness among users of the
correct usage of these tools must be considered to avoid
the spread of rumours and false information during flood
emergencies (Alexander,
2013; Reuter & Kaufhold, 2018).
In this regard, one possible step to overcome these short-
comings is recruiting local official volunteers. Limiting
communication and sharing functionalities to these pre-
identified groups can help improve the quality and reli-
ability of collected data (Tavra et al.,
2021). Additional
concerns regarding their implementation include the need
for advanced algorithms and skilled staff to correctly vali-
date the information generated (Rossi et al.,
2018).
Although the results suggest an overall positive evalu-
ation, this should be seen as an initial exploratory assess-
ment of the current A4alerts version and its
functionalities within the context of flood events. Real-
time evaluations must be performed to move from the
perceived usefulness and intention of the participants to
use the A4alerts app and its functionalities towards
actual behaviour. Moreover, additional research is
needed to determine the relationships and influence of
variables such as individual functional appropriateness
and usefulness of the A4alerts features with the actual
adoption and acceptance rate of users. Nevertheless, the
results for the A4alerts prototype and its functionalities
are promising and serve as a base for validating future
evaluations and development iterations to support the
protective actions of end-users during flood emergencies.

6 | LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE
RESEARCH
As part of people-centred designs, user experience evalu-
ations are essential to explore how individuals perceive
and interact with technological tools (Asikin-Garmager
et al., 2022). Nevertheless, due to the complexity of pro-
viding robust prototypes for these evaluations, previous
research on crisis apps has used limited in-person experi-
ences with low-fidelity prototypes or paper-based app
demonstrations (Colombelli et al.,
2020; Kaufhold
et al., 2018). To further advance the field of hazard crisis
apps, this study employed an operational and ready-to-
be-used prototype of the A4alerts to evaluate the imple-
mented functionalities from an end-user perspective.
In the context of the ongoing COVID-19 restrictions in
early 2022, a strategic decision was made to conduct the
evaluation within an online experimental environment,
utilizing a web-based version of the app. This adjustment
facilitated the broader dissemination of the demo within
the different groups of stakeholders of Terrassa and Cata-
lonia. However, the demo was not publicly accessible but
exclusively distributed via email through the civil protec-
tion internal communication network of Catalonia. Conse-
quently, the scope of responses was confined to these
specific groups ( n = 76). This limited reach, alongside
inherent technological barriers (e.g., compatibility issues,
stable internet access), could have resulted in fewer partic-
ipants accessing and engaging with the demo. Moreover,
the online experience limited the interaction with the par-
ticipants to provide extra support or answer any questions
related to the app or demo. Although a short video about
the A4alerts was presented (in sections one and three of
the demo), the restricted communication can also explain
why most participants considered that additional guidance
and training must be given to correctly use the app.
Accordingly, additional workshops and in-person demon-
strations should be conducted to build upon the existing
findings. This approach will enable gathering first-hand
experiences, interactions, and opinions from participants
as they actively engage with the A4alerts application.
Finally, evaluating the A4alerts and its functionalities
within the experimental demo is based on using beha-
vioural intentions as a proxy for actual behaviours
(Ajzen,
1985; Weyrich et al., 2020). Although a positive
correlation between these two variables has been demon-
strated in prior studies (Armitage & Conner, 2001), the
intention to use a crisis app and its functionalities
(e.g., sharing and validation) during emergencies can be
influenced by several factors, such as trust in the system,
risk perception and self-efficacy (Fischer et al.,
2019).
Real-time evaluation periods are needed to move from
the initial exploratory user assessment of the A4alerts
app and its functionalities to understand how it can be
operationalised to support emergency responses and
enable wider community acceptance. In this context,
operational demonstrations of the A4alerts and its future
iterations will be carried out in the next five years across
municipalities in Catalonia (more than 20) and Europe

7 | CONCLUSIONS
Communities are demanding that risk information be
quickly available and accessible, especially when the time
window to perform local protection actions is limited.
The widespread use of smartphones has opened the door
for specialised hazard crisis apps to be a valuable comple-
ment to the rapid dissemination of flood warnings during
emergencies. This research presents the A4alerts, an
innovative flood-focused crisis mobile app developed to
display the information from the community-based
SS-EWS.
A first user experience evaluation of the A4alerts app
and its functionalities within the context of flood scenar-
ios was performed with Terrassa and Catalonia commu-
nity representatives through an online experimental
demo. The overall results suggest that the A4alerts was
considered a potentially useful tool to effectively support
users and their actions during flood emergencies. How-
ever, the results also indicate that resources should be
allocated to provide supplementary training and guid-
ance on how to successfully use the app. Furthermore,
the functionalities implemented in the A4alerts were
ranked by participants from the most to the least useful
during emergencies. The SSWs, official warnings, and the
self-protection action checklist were ranked as the most
useful functionalities. Although innovative functionali-
ties to enable the participation of end-users in providing
and validating impact information were not among the
first ranked, results suggest participants are willing to
use them.
Understanding what and how to efficiently integrate
technological applications and local needs for emergency
communication is not a straightforward, one-way recipe.
Nevertheless, the present evaluation of the A4alerts app
can contribute to understanding the benefits, limitations,
and areas of work within the field and as an example of
good practice for other regions and communities. In this
sense, the A4alerts app can have a supporting role in dis-
seminating crucial local and impact-based warning infor-
mation and breaching the communication gap between
authorities, responders, and the most vulnerable commu-
nities during flood emergencies.
